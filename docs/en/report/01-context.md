# Context

## The rise of e-commerce and its logistics impact

E-commerce has ceased to be an alternative channel and has become the central axis of modern supply chains. Between 2019 and 2024, global online sales grew steadily, surpassing pre-pandemic projections and consolidating a market that demands logistics capacity at an unprecedented scale [1][2]. In Mexico, the Hot Sale 2025 event recorded a 23.7% growth over the previous year, with sales of $42,725 million pesos — a figure that illustrates how quickly digital consumption turns into pressure on warehouse and distribution operations [5].


![Context Diagram 1]({{ "/assets/img/Comercio Minorista.png" | relative_url }})

This acceleration is not neutral for logistics. The three operations most sensitive to volume — picking, packing, and sortation — concentrate the largest share of labor costs and errors in distribution centers. When order volumes scale and promised delivery times shrink, demand variability becomes the main operational risk factor: the logistics system must absorb peaks of up to three or four times the average volume without degrading service quality.

## The e-commerce logistics cycle and its critical points

The lifecycle of an online order comprises well-defined stages: demand capture, processing, warehouse fulfillment, packaging, dispatch, transit, and, where applicable, returns. Each transition introduces latency and potential for error. The fulfillment stage is the most operationally critical: it involves movement within the warehouse, precise product identification, physical retrieval of the item, verification, and recording — all under time pressure and with direct consequences for customer satisfaction if something fails [3].

![E-commerce lifecycle]({{ "/assets/img/Ciclo de vida del E-commerce.png" | relative_url }})

In highly variable environments, human operational capacity reaches its limit relatively quickly. The conventional solution has been to hire temporary staff during seasonal peaks, which introduces training costs, a higher error rate due to inexperience, and high turnover. The underlying problem is not one of one-time scale, but of systemic design: traditional warehouses are not equipped to absorb demand fluctuations without sacrificing quality or incurring extraordinary costs.

## Automation as the industry's response

The response of the global logistics industry has been the progressive automation of distribution centers. The global warehouse automation market — which includes autonomous guided vehicles (AGV/AMR), automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS), robotic arms, and sortation platforms — was estimated at $15,000 million dollars in 2019 and is projected to reach $30,000 million by 2026 [4]. This sustained growth reflects both the technological maturity of the available solutions and the economic pressure on logistics operations to reduce their dependence on labor for repetitive and physically demanding tasks.

However, full warehouse automation — the most efficient solution in terms of productivity — requires capital investments that only large operators can afford. For the universe of medium and small logistics operations, and for those in emerging markets where the cost of labor does not yet justify full automation, there is a middle ground: collaborative robotization, where the robot extends the human operator's capabilities rather than replacing them. This project is situated in that space, with the additional condition that the operator does not need to be physically present in the warehouse.
