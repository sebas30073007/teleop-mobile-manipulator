# Problem Statement

## Logistics under pressure and the inclusion gap

The digital transformation of commerce has produced an operational paradox: while order volumes grow at an accelerated pace, the labor structure of warehouses has not evolved at the same rate. Fulfillment, internal transfer, and restocking tasks continue to be labor-intensive, physically demanding, and difficult to perform from anywhere other than the warehouse floor. Dependence on the operator's physical presence is the main operational constraint that current logistics has not resolved.

![Logistics and last-mile costs]({{ "/assets/img/Costos logisticos.png" | relative_url }})

In parallel, Mexico has a population of 9.5 million people with some form of disability who face systemic barriers to accessing formal employment [7][8]. The informal labor rate of this population exceeds 60%, which does not reflect a lack of cognitive or attentional capabilities, but rather a structural absence of work tools adapted to their conditions [7].

![Disability]({{ "/assets/img/Discapacidad.png" | relative_url }})

## Two converging curves

The problem can be read as two intersecting trends, and their intersection defines the opportunity that this project seeks to exploit:

**Curve 1 — Unmet logistics demand:** Distribution centers need flexible, reliable operational capacity with a low margin of error in repetitive tasks. Temporary hiring solves the volume problem but not the quality problem. Full automation solves the quality problem but is not within reach of every operation, and it eliminates jobs without generating alternatives for displaced operator profiles.

**Curve 2 — Talent with disabilities lacking a productive access channel:** People with motor disabilities possess cognitive, attentional, and precision capabilities that are valuable in supervision, remote control, and system operation tasks. However, the lack of adapted tools — accessible interfaces, assistance systems, remote work environments — prevents that talent from being productively incorporated into formal value chains.

The intersection space between these two curves is inclusion-oriented robotic teleoperation: a remote operator controls a physical system in the warehouse from an interface designed for their capabilities, executing concrete logistics tasks with precision, without needing physical presence in the work environment and without requiring mobility infrastructure that many people with disabilities do not have.


## Why existing solutions do not close the gap

The automation solutions available today — industrial robots, AGVs, AS/RS systems — are fundamentally designed to *replace* the human operator in repetitive tasks, not to *enable* them from a remote location. From a labor-inclusion perspective, this orientation worsens the problem: it reduces the total number of jobs in the sector without generating accessible alternatives for people with disabilities.

On the other hand, conventional telework platforms (videoconferencing, desktop interfaces, digital collaboration) do not allow physical interaction with the warehouse environment: they remain limited to administrative tasks or passive supervision. There is no technological bridge between the operational capacity that a person with a motor disability has in front of a computer and the execution of a physical task in a warehouse.

The gap this project seeks to address is, consequently, specific and technically approachable: the design of a system that connects the remote operator's intent with the robot's physical action in the warehouse, with an interface accessible enough to be operable by people with reduced mobility and with technical performance robust enough to be useful under real logistics conditions.
